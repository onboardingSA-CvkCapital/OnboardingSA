Government Job Interviews: Common Questions and How to Answer Them

A government interview is usually a panel, a fixed set of questions and a scoring sheet. Once you understand how it works, you can prepare for it properly.

In short

  • Government interviews are usually run by a panel that asks every candidate the same questions and scores each answer.
  • Questions usually cover the department, the duties in the advert, your experience, and competencies such as teamwork, ethics and problem-solving.
  • Answer competency questions with a real example: the situation, what you did and the result.
  • Bring your ID and the original or certified documents the invitation asks for, and arrive early.

Being invited to a government interview is a real achievement. For many posts, hundreds of people apply and only a handful are shortlisted. But the interview itself can feel intimidating: a long table, a panel of strangers, a list of questions read out word for word, and people writing down scores as you speak.

The good news is that government interviews follow a predictable pattern. Once you understand how the panel works and what kinds of questions it asks, you can prepare thoroughly. This guide explains the process, the common questions and how to answer them well.

How a government interview works

Public service recruitment follows rules designed to make selection fair and consistent. In practice, this usually means:

  • A panel, not one person. Several people interview you together. Typically there is a chairperson, the manager of the post, other senior staff and someone from HR. A union representative may attend as an observer.
  • The same questions for everyone. The panel prepares questions in advance and asks every candidate the same set, so answers can be compared fairly.
  • Scoring. Each panel member scores your answers, usually against expected points they agreed before the interviews started.
  • Limited conversation. Because questions are fixed, the panel may not follow up much on your answers. That means each answer needs to be complete on its own.

Knowing this changes how you answer. The panel is listening for specific points. If you cover them clearly, you score. If you give a vague or very short answer, you may lose points even if you know the subject well.

The kinds of questions you will be asked

1. Opening questions

Panels usually start with an easy question to settle you, such as "Tell us about yourself" or "Briefly take us through your CV". Prepare a two-minute answer: your qualification, your relevant experience in order, and why you are interested in this post. Do not tell your life story, and do not read your CV aloud.

2. Questions about the department

Panels often ask what you know about the department or the unit you would join. For example: "What is the mandate of this department?" or "What are the strategic priorities of this branch?" Prepare by reading the department's website, especially its vision and mission, its annual performance plan and any recent news. Know what the unit you are applying to does.

3. Questions about the duties in the advert

Many questions come directly from the duties listed in the advert. If the advert says "Render administrative support to the directorate", expect a question like "How would you ensure effective administrative support in the directorate?" Go through the advert's duties one by one and prepare what you would do, the systems and procedures involved, and an example from your own experience for each.

4. Technical and legislative questions

For many posts, panels ask about the laws, policies and systems you would work with. Depending on the post, these may include:

  • The Public Finance Management Act (PFMA) and Treasury regulations, for finance and supply chain posts.
  • The Public Service Act and Public Service Regulations, for HR and administration posts.
  • The Labour Relations Act and Basic Conditions of Employment Act, for HR and labour relations posts.
  • The Promotion of Access to Information Act (PAIA) and Protection of Personal Information Act (POPIA), for records and information posts.
  • Systems such as PERSAL for personnel and salaries, BAS for accounting and LOGIS for procurement and stores, where they apply.
  • Sector-specific laws, such as health, education, correctional services or environmental legislation.

The advert's "knowledge" section is your guide. If it lists a law or a system, expect a question on it. You do not need to memorise every section, but you should know what the law is for, the main principles, and how it affects the work of the post.

5. Competency questions

These ask you to describe how you behaved in real situations. They usually start with "Tell us about a time when..." or "Give an example of...". Common themes include:

  • Working in a team, and dealing with a difficult colleague.
  • Handling a complaint from a member of the public.
  • Working under pressure or with tight deadlines.
  • Solving a problem or improving a process.
  • Dealing with an ethical dilemma, such as being offered a bribe or seeing a colleague break the rules.
  • Planning and organising your work.
  • For supervisory posts: managing poor performance, delegating and motivating a team.

6. Scenario questions

Scenario questions describe a situation and ask what you would do. For example: "A member of the public arrives angry because their application has been delayed for three months. How would you handle it?" Panels are listening for professionalism, empathy, knowledge of the correct procedure and an appropriate escalation if needed.

7. Closing questions

At the end, the panel may ask "Do you have any questions for us?" or "Is there anything you would like to add?" It is fine to ask a short question about the post, such as what the main priorities would be in the first few months. You can also use the moment to add a strong point you did not get to mention. Avoid asking about salary or leave at this stage; the advert already states the salary.

How to answer competency questions: STAR

The most reliable method for competency questions is STAR:

  1. Situation. Set the scene briefly: where you worked and what was happening.
  2. Task. Explain what you were responsible for.
  3. Action. Describe what you did, step by step. This is the most important part.
  4. Result. Say what happened in the end, ideally with a number or concrete outcome, and what you learned.

Here is an example answer to "Tell us about a time you handled a difficult member of the public":

Example STAR answer"At the municipal revenue office where I worked as a clerk, a resident came in very upset because his water account showed a huge amount he did not believe he owed (situation). My job was to help him and resolve the query (task). I took him to a quieter desk, listened without interrupting, and then pulled his account history on the system. I found that an estimated reading had been used for four months instead of the actual meter reading. I explained this to him, logged a meter reading request and a query with the billing section, and gave him the reference number and my extension (action). The account was corrected within two weeks and he phoned to thank us. Since then I check for estimated readings first whenever someone queries a high bill (result)."

Prepare five or six STAR stories from your own experience before the interview. Many can be adapted to different questions. If you are new to work, use examples from your studies, volunteering, part-time jobs or community involvement.

Batho Pele and public service values

Many government panels ask about Batho Pele, which means "People First". It is the public service's set of principles for serving the public, and covers ideas such as consultation, service standards, access, courtesy, information, openness and transparency, redress and value for money. You may be asked to name the principles, or to explain how you would apply them in the post.

Panels also care about the values in the Constitution's basic principles for public administration, such as professional ethics, efficiency, impartiality, accountability and transparency. Prepare an example of how you have applied these values in your own work.

Preparing in the days before

  • Reread the advert and turn each duty and knowledge item into a possible question.
  • Research the department: its mandate, priorities and the unit you would join.
  • Revise the relevant laws and systems listed in the advert.
  • Prepare your STAR stories and practise saying them out loud.
  • Check the invitation for the date, time, venue, any test, and which documents to bring.
  • Plan your route and aim to arrive at least 30 minutes early. Government buildings often have security checks at the entrance.

On the day

  • Bring what the invitation asks for, usually your ID and the originals or certified copies of your qualifications. Our certified copies guide explains what makes a copy valid.
  • Dress neatly and professionally.
  • Greet the panel and wait to be invited to sit.
  • Listen to the whole question. If it has several parts, write them down and answer each part.
  • Take a moment before answering if you need to. A short pause is better than a rushed, unclear answer.
  • Speak to the whole panel, not only the person who asked the question.
  • Be honest. If you do not know something, say so and explain how you would find out.

After the interview

After interviews, the panel recommends a candidate and the appointment goes through approval, which can take time. Reference checks, qualification verification and security vetting may happen in this period. If you are not successful, you can politely ask the HR contact whether feedback is available, although not every department provides it. Either way, each interview is practice for the next one.

Questions people ask

How many people are on a government interview panel?

Panels usually have several members, often including the manager of the post, a senior official, an HR representative and sometimes a union observer. The exact make-up depends on the department and the level of the post.

Will there be a test as well as an interview?

Sometimes. Many departments use a written, practical or computer test for some posts, and senior management posts usually include a competency assessment. The invitation normally tells you if there will be a test.

Can I bring notes into the interview?

You can usually bring a pen and a notepad, and it is fine to jot down a long question. Reading prepared answers from paper is not a good idea, because the panel wants to hear you speak naturally.

How long does it take to hear back?

It varies widely. Some departments make appointments within weeks, others take months, because appointments go through approval steps. If you have not heard back after the period the department mentions, you may assume you were not successful, but keep applying for other posts.
