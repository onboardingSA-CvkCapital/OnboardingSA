Bank Jobs in South Africa: Entry Routes at Capitec, FNB and Standard Bank

Banks hire thousands of people every year, from branch and call centre staff to data analysts and software engineers. Here is how to get in, and the checks you need to be ready for.

In short

  • Each bank recruits through its own careers site. There are no fees, agents or WhatsApp recruiters.
  • Many bank posts require a clear criminal record and a clear credit record, and the bank will check both.
  • Advice and sales roles in financial products often require the RE5 regulatory exam, or completing it within a set time.
  • Graduate programmes are the main entry route for people with degrees in finance, IT, data and related fields.

Banking is one of South Africa's largest private-sector employers. The big banks run branches in almost every town, large contact centres, and head offices full of specialists in finance, risk, data, technology, marketing and legal work. For job seekers, that means a wide range of opportunities: entry-level service roles for people with a matric, graduate programmes for people with degrees, and specialist posts for experienced professionals.

Banks are also careful employers. Because they handle money and personal information, many posts come with background checks that surprise first-time applicants. This guide explains where bank jobs are advertised, the main entry routes, the checks you should expect, and how to prepare a strong application.

Where bank jobs are advertised

Each bank recruits through its own official careers site, and applications are made there:

  • Capitec advertises on its own careers site. Its head office is in Stellenbosch in the Western Cape, and it has branches and service centres across the country.
  • FirstRand (the group behind FNB, RMB and WesBank) advertises across its brands on one careers site.
  • Standard Bank advertises South African posts on its official careers site.
  • Other banks and insurers follow a similar pattern, each with its own portal.

Some bank adverts appear on many job boards as well, but the application always ends up on the bank's own system. If anyone asks you to send your CV to a personal email address or WhatsApp number for a bank job, or asks for a fee, treat it as a scam. Our guide on how to tell if a job advert is real explains the warning signs.

Entry routes into banking

Branch and service roles

Branch consultants, service consultants, tellers and client advisors are the face of the bank. They open accounts, help clients with transactions and problems, and sell basic products. These posts often ask for Grade 12, good communication skills, computer literacy and customer service experience. Some also ask for mathematics or accounting at school, or a relevant post-matric certificate.

Contact centre roles

Contact centres handle calls, chats and messages from clients. Many entry-level jobs in banking are here, and they are often a good first step because you learn the bank's products and systems quickly. Expect to be assessed on communication, listening, typing and problem-solving. Shift work is common.

Sales and advice roles

Some roles involve selling or advising on financial products, such as investments, insurance or wealth services. These are regulated under the Financial Advisory and Intermediary Services (FAIS) Act, and the advert may require you to have passed the RE5 regulatory exam, or to complete it within a set period after you start. Some adverts also mention credit-related requirements under the National Credit Act.

Graduate programmes

The banks run graduate programmes for people who have recently completed degrees in fields such as accounting, finance, economics, actuarial science, data science, IT, engineering and law. These programmes usually include rotations, training and mentoring over one to two years. They are competitive and often open once a year, so watch the bank's careers site and campus recruitment announcements. Our graduate programmes guide covers how they work.

Specialist and experienced posts

Many adverts are for experienced people: data analysts, software engineers, risk and compliance specialists, accountants, credit analysts, HR specialists and managers. These usually require a degree or diploma, several years of relevant experience, and specific technical skills listed in the advert.

The checks banks run

This is where many applicants are caught off guard. Bank adverts commonly state conditions such as "clear criminal and credit record" or "candidates will require a credit record check in accordance with the National Credit Act".

  • Criminal record. A criminal record, particularly for dishonesty offences, can rule you out of many banking posts.
  • Credit record. Judgments, serious arrears or other adverse listings can be a problem, especially for posts that involve credit or financial advice. If you know you have issues, it is worth getting your free annual credit report from a credit bureau and dealing with problems before you apply.
  • Qualification verification. Banks verify the qualifications you list. Never claim a qualification you do not have.
  • Fit and proper requirements. For regulated roles, banks must check that representatives meet honesty, integrity and competence standards.
Be honest upfrontIf an application form asks about your credit or criminal history, answer truthfully. Banks place a very high value on honesty, and a false answer discovered during checks is far more damaging than the issue itself.

Reading a bank advert

Bank adverts often follow a pattern: a short introduction to the bank or the team, a description of the role, a list of responsibilities, and a section on what they are looking for. FNB adverts, for example, often use headings like "Are you someone who can:" for duties and "You will be an ideal candidate if you:" for requirements. Capitec adverts typically list a purpose statement, experience, qualifications, knowledge, skills and conditions of employment.

Look out for:

  • The closing date, which may be shown at the end of the advert rather than the top.
  • Conditions of employment, such as credit checks, being contactable by phone, or shift work.
  • Whether it is a real vacancy or a talent pool. Some adverts say plainly that they are building a pool for future roles.
  • The location, especially for branch roles, which are tied to a specific branch or region.

How to make your application stronger

  1. Match your CV to the advert. Use the bank's own words where they are true for you. If the advert asks for "experience in a sales and service environment", say exactly that and give dates.
  2. Show numbers where you can. In sales or service roles, mention targets you met, customer satisfaction scores or the volume of clients you handled.
  3. Highlight trust. Mention roles where you handled cash, confidential information or stock. Banks want people they can trust.
  4. Prepare for assessments. Many banks use online assessments for numeracy, verbal reasoning and personality. Practise similar tests online, and take them somewhere quiet with a stable connection.
  5. Write a short cover note if the system allows one. Our cover letter guide shows how to keep it short and specific.

Interviews at banks

Bank interviews are often competency-based: the interviewer asks for examples of situations you have handled. Common themes include dealing with an angry customer, meeting a target under pressure, making a mistake and fixing it, and noticing something suspicious. Use the situation, task, action and result structure to answer. For specialist roles, expect technical questions or a case study.

Research the bank before the interview: its main products, its brand promise and any recent news. Interviewers notice when candidates understand what the bank stands for and how the role fits in.

Questions people ask

Why do banks check my credit record?

Banks handle money and sensitive financial information, and many of their posts involve giving financial advice or credit. Under financial sector rules and the banks' own policies, staff in many roles must be fit and proper, which includes credit and criminal checks. Adverts usually say when a credit record check applies.

What is RE5?

RE5 is a regulatory examination for representatives who give advice or render services on certain financial products under the FAIS Act. Some bank adverts list it as a requirement; others say you must complete it within a set period after appointment.

Can I work at a bank without a degree?

Yes. Many branch, service, sales and contact centre posts require Grade 12, sometimes with mathematics or a post-matric certificate. Experience in customer service or sales helps. Specialist posts in finance, IT, risk and data usually require a degree or diploma.

Some bank adverts say they are for a 'talent pool'. What does that mean?

A talent pool advert collects applications for future vacancies rather than one specific open post. You may be contacted later when a suitable post opens. Treat it as a bonus, and keep applying for specific vacancies too.
