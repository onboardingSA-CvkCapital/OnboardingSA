Learnerships vs Internships vs Apprenticeships

They sound similar, but they're not the same — and choosing the right one can shape your whole career. Here's exactly what each means in South Africa, who qualifies, how the money works, and how to apply.

Career Guides · OnboardingSA

If you're young and looking for your first real step into work, you'll see three words over and over again: learnership, internship, and apprenticeship. Recruiters use them loosely, adverts mix them up, and it's easy to apply for the wrong thing — or to skip the right one because you didn't understand it.

All three combine working and learning at the same time, and all three can lead to a permanent job. But they suit different people, they pay differently, they last for very different lengths of time, and they end with different results on your CV. Getting this right matters, because the programme you choose now decides what doors open for you in three, five and ten years. This guide breaks each one down clearly, then shows you how to actually find and apply for them.

Learnerships

A learnership is a structured programme that combines classroom-style learning with practical work experience, and it leads to a qualification that is registered on the National Qualifications Framework (NQF). Learnerships fall under the Skills Development Act and are run through the various SETAs — the Sector Education and Training Authorities, each of which covers an industry (for example, banking, manufacturing, health and welfare, wholesale and retail, transport, and construction).

What makes a learnership different from just a training course is the three-way agreement behind it: you, the employer, and an accredited training provider all sign on. You spend part of your time being trained and part of it doing real work for the employer, and at the end you sit an assessment that, if you pass, earns you a full, recognised qualification.

  • Who it's for: Both unemployed young people wanting a foot in the door, and people already employed who want a formal qualification in their field.
  • How long: Usually around 12 months, though some run longer depending on the NQF level.
  • Pay: A monthly stipend (an allowance), not a full salary. The amount is set by the employer and the relevant SETA and varies a lot by sector and level — always check the advert.
  • Entry level: Many need matric, but some are aimed at people without Grade 12, and there are lower-NQF programmes built to give foundational skills.
  • The outcome: A recognised NQF qualification plus real work experience — a strong combination on any CV.
Good to know: Because a learnership ends in a formal qualification, it's especially valuable if you couldn't study further after matric. You earn a little while you learn, and you walk away with a certificate that employers across the country recognise — not just a letter from one company.

Internships

An internship gives you practical experience in your field, usually after — or near the end of — a qualification you're already doing. Where a learnership is built around earning a new qualification, an internship is about applying one you already have and turning theory into real, on-the-job ability. It's the classic answer to the frustrating "we need someone with experience" problem: an internship is how you get that first experience.

  • Who it's for: Graduates, diploma holders and final-year students who have the theory but need workplace experience and references.
  • How long: Commonly 6 to 24 months.
  • Pay: Usually a stipend. Some internships (especially in the private sector) pay well; others pay very little — confirm the amount before you commit.
  • The outcome: Experience, references, professional contacts, and often a direct path to a permanent role at the same organisation.

Government also runs large graduate internship programmes through national and provincial departments, and many of these are advertised alongside ordinary vacancies. They're competitive, so a clean, well-targeted application matters.

Apprenticeships

An apprenticeship trains you in a skilled trade — electrician, boilermaker, fitter and turner, welder, diesel mechanic, plumber, millwright. It combines on-the-job training under a qualified artisan with technical education, and it builds towards a trade test. Pass the trade test and you become a qualified artisan, holding a nationally recognised trade certificate (often called a Red Seal).

  • Who it's for: People who want a hands-on, technical career and long-term earning power in a trade.
  • How long: Typically 3 to 4 years — the longest of the three, because you're mastering a full trade.
  • Pay: An allowance that usually increases as you move through the stages of the apprenticeship.
  • Entry level: Trades often expect maths and science subjects, and many artisans come through a TVET college N-course route before or during their apprenticeship.
  • The outcome: A qualified artisan trade certificate. Skilled artisans are in real demand in South Africa and can earn very well — and the qualification travels, including overseas.
Worth knowing: An apprenticeship is a longer commitment, but it's one of the most reliable routes to a stable, well-paid career in South Africa. Qualified artisans are consistently on the country's scarce-skills lists.

Which one is right for you?

  • Want a formal qualification while working, even without further study? Go for a learnership.
  • Already qualified and just need real workplace experience? Go for an internship.
  • Want a hands-on trade and strong long-term earning power? Go for an apprenticeship.

If you're genuinely unsure, ask yourself one question: do I need a qualification, or do I need experience? If you need the qualification, lean learnership or apprenticeship. If you already have the paper and need the practice, lean internship.

Documents you'll usually need

Have these ready before opportunities open, because closing dates come fast and a missing document is the most common reason good applicants get rejected:

  • A certified copy of your ID
  • Your matric certificate or latest results (and any further qualifications)
  • A short, clean CV
  • Certified copies of any certificates or transcripts
  • Sometimes proof of residence and a completed application form (government programmes often use their own forms)
Tip: Get a fresh set of certified copies every few months — many offices will only accept certifications that are less than three months old.

How and where to find them

These programmes are advertised in a few predictable places:

  • SETA websites — each SETA publishes learnerships and grants for its sector.
  • Government job portals — national and provincial departments advertise internships, learnerships and apprenticeships, often early in the year, with strict closing dates.
  • Large employers' career pages — banks, retailers, mines, manufacturers and utilities run structured youth programmes.
  • TVET colleges — a common entry route into apprenticeships and trades.
  • OnboardingSA — we pull current opportunities into one place and link each one straight to the official application.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Missing the closing date. These deadlines are strict and rarely extended. Apply early, not on the last day.
  • Paying a "fee". Legitimate learnerships, internships and apprenticeships do not charge you to apply or to be placed. Anyone asking for money is a red flag.
  • Sending the same CV everywhere. Match your CV to what the advert actually asks for.
  • Treating it as "just training". These are real jobs and real auditions — show up on time, work hard, and be someone people want to keep.

Frequently asked questions

Do you get paid on a learnership?

Yes, but it's a stipend (an allowance), not a full salary. The amount is set by the employer and the relevant SETA and varies by sector and NQF level. It's meant to help with transport and basic costs while you learn.

Can you do a learnership without matric?

Sometimes. Many learnerships require matric, but others are designed for people who didn't complete Grade 12, and there are lower-NQF programmes built to give foundational skills. Always check the entry requirements on the specific advert.

What's the real difference between a learnership and an apprenticeship?

A learnership can be in almost any sector and leads to an NQF-registered qualification, usually over about a year. An apprenticeship is specifically for a skilled trade, takes about three to four years, and ends in a trade test that qualifies you as an artisan.

Do these programmes lead to permanent jobs?

There's no guarantee, but many do. Employers often keep strong participants on, and even when they don't, you leave with a recognised qualification or solid experience, references, and contacts — which makes your next application far stronger.
