Joining the South African Police Service is one of the most popular career goals in the country. A trainee post offers a monthly stipend while you train, a permanent salary once you finish, medical aid and a pension, and a job that is genuinely needed in almost every community. That popularity is also the problem: for every intake, SAPS receives far more applications than it has places, so the process is strict and unforgiving. A missing copy, a second application or a fitness test you did not prepare for can end your chances before anyone has even met you.

This guide explains the whole journey in plain language: who qualifies, how to fill in and hand in the application, what happens at each selection stage, what basic training looks like, and the mistakes that knock out good candidates every year. It is based on the official SAPS trainee advertisements and the way the intake has been run in recent years. The details for each new intake are published by SAPS, so always read the current advert alongside this guide.

What the police trainee programme is

The trainee programme is how SAPS recruits new entry-level police officials, the people who become constables at police stations around the country. You do not need any previous policing experience. Instead, SAPS selects people who meet the minimum requirements and pass its assessments, and then trains them from the beginning.

The programme is formally called the Basic Police Development Learning Programme. In recent intakes it has run for about 21 months, made up of three parts:

  1. Induction of about one month at a designated police station, where you get your first look at how a station works.
  2. Basic training at a SAPS academy. Recent adverts list about eight months for matriculants and graduates in other fields, and about six months for law graduates.
  3. Probation of about twelve months of workplace exposure at a station, where you work under supervision before you are confirmed in the post.

During basic training you receive a monthly stipend, and SAPS provides training gear, meals, accommodation and medical cover. Once you move to probation you are paid a salary as an entry-level member. The exact amounts are set for each intake; the 2024/2025 advert, for example, listed a stipend of R4,500 a month during training and a salary of R216,033 a year during probation, plus benefits.

Two kinds of SAPS jobsThe trainee intake is for police officials employed under the Police Act. SAPS also employs civilians, such as admin clerks, cleaners, drivers and IT staff, under the Public Service Act. Those posts are advertised separately and are applied for differently, often with a Z83 form. If you want to wear the uniform and do police work, the trainee intake is the route.

Who can apply: the minimum requirements

SAPS sets minimum requirements for every intake. If you miss even one, your application is not considered, so check each one carefully before you start. Based on recent official adverts, you must:

  • Be a South African citizen with permanent residence in South Africa, and be able to prove it.
  • Be at least 18 and under 35 years old.
  • Have a Grade 12 (matric) certificate or an equivalent qualification.
  • Be proficient in English and at least one other official language.
  • Have no previous criminal convictions, and agree to fingerprinting and a background check.
  • Not be listed on the National Register for Sex Offenders or the Child Protection Register.
  • Not have been dismissed from a government department for misconduct.
  • Be physically and mentally fit, and pass the medical examination.
  • Be willing to take the oath of office, complete the full training, and serve anywhere in South Africa.

Recent adverts have also listed physical standards that surprise many applicants. A body mass index (BMI) of 30 or lower was required, along with a maximum waist measurement (102 cm for men and 88 cm for women in the 2024/2025 advert). Visible tattoos that show when you wear the uniform were not allowed. People with chronic conditions that could interrupt training may be excluded, and hiding a condition can lead to your application being withdrawn later.

There are also rules about other work. Recent adverts said that trainees may not do paid work in the taxi, liquor or security industries, and that anyone with a business that does business with the state must give up that directorship.

Requirements that help but are not compulsory

A valid driver's licence for a light motor vehicle has been listed as an advantage. Tertiary qualifications, especially in law, policing or related fields, can also count in your favour, and law graduates have had a shorter academy programme. None of these replaces the minimum requirements above, but they can make a strong application stronger.

How to apply, step by step

The application itself is simple, but SAPS is strict about how it is done. Follow these steps exactly.

  1. Wait for the official advert. SAPS announces each intake on its own website and through official government channels. Recruitment is not open all year round. If someone says they can get you in outside an official intake, it is a scam.
  2. Get the official SAPS application form. It is free at police stations, SAPS recruitment offices and on the SAPS careers pages. Use the version for the current intake.
  3. Fill in every section clearly. Use black ink, write neatly and answer every question. Where something does not apply to you, write "N/A" rather than leaving it blank.
  4. Attach the documents the advert asks for. Recent intakes asked for copies of your ID, matric certificate, any tertiary qualifications and academic record, your driver's licence if you have one, testimonials, and service certificates from previous employers if you have worked before. Read the advert for the current intake to see whether copies must be certified.
  5. Hand it in at the right place before the closing date. Recent adverts asked applicants to hand-deliver applications to their nearest police station during office hours. Late applications are not accepted, however good the reason.
  6. Apply only once. You may submit one application per intake. Submitting more than one, for example at two different stations, can disqualify you completely.
Read the advert twice: once to understand it, and once with a pen in your hand, ticking off every requirement and every document.

Some intakes and some specialised police posts are handled through the SAPS e-Recruitment portal instead of on paper. If the advert for your intake tells you to apply online, do that and do not also hand in a paper form. Follow whichever method the current advert gives, because that is the only one that counts.

The selection process: what happens after you apply

If your application meets the minimum requirements, you move into selection. Not everyone who qualifies is invited, because there are usually far more qualifying applicants than places. Those who are invited go through several stages. Recent adverts listed them in roughly this order.

Fitness, psychometric and integrity testing

The fitness assessment checks whether you can cope with the physical side of training. It typically includes exercises such as push-ups, sit-ups and a timed run. Many candidates fail here simply because they arrive unprepared. If you are serious about joining, start training months before the intake opens: run three or four times a week, build up your push-ups and sit-ups gradually, and practise until you can do the exercises with good form while tired.

The psychometric and integrity tests look at how you think, how you handle pressure and how you respond to ethical situations. You cannot really study for them, but you can arrive rested, read each question carefully, answer honestly and not rush. Trying to guess the "right" answer usually backfires, because these tests are designed to pick up inconsistent answers.

Medical evaluation

The medical checks whether you are fit for training and for police work. This is where BMI, waist measurement, eyesight and chronic conditions are assessed. Be honest on the medical forms. Hiding a condition does not make it disappear, and it can lead to your appointment being withdrawn later.

Interview

Shortlisted candidates attend an interview, usually with a panel. Expect questions about why you want to join the police, what you understand about the role of a police officer, how you would handle difficult or dangerous situations, and situations from your own life where you showed responsibility, honesty or teamwork. Our guide to common public-sector interview questions explains how to prepare answers that are clear and specific.

Verification and vetting

SAPS checks your qualifications, driver's licence, citizenship and address, and runs a security screening that includes reference checks and fingerprint verification. Recent adverts also mentioned a buccal (inside-cheek) DNA sample for the forensic database. Anything you wrote on your form that turns out to be false can end your application at this stage, even if you passed everything else.

Waiting to hear backSAPS contacts only the candidates who move forward. If you have not heard anything within about three months after the closing date, take it that you were not successful this time. Being selected for the next stage also does not guarantee appointment; only a final offer does.

Life at the academy: what basic training is like

Basic training happens at one of the SAPS academies around the country. It is a residential programme: you live at the academy, follow a fixed daily routine and wear uniform. The training combines classroom learning with practical work. You can expect to study law and legal procedure, police powers and duties, human rights, crime prevention, handling complaints and statements, and the use of firearms, along with physical training, drill and discipline.

Trainees often say the hardest part is not the physical training but the discipline and pace. Days start early, rules are strict and you are assessed throughout. People who arrive fit, organised and ready to take instruction tend to cope best. If you struggle with academic work, start reading now: practise summarising what you read and writing clear, short paragraphs, because statements and reports are a big part of police work.

After the academy you go back to a police station for probation, where you work alongside experienced members. This is when you learn how theory becomes practice: attending to complaints, taking statements, doing patrols and working shifts that include nights, weekends and public holidays. When you complete probation successfully, you are confirmed in your post as a member of the Service.

Mistakes that cost people their place

Most applicants who are turned away do not fail because they would make bad police officers. They fail because of avoidable mistakes. These are the ones that come up again and again.

  • Applying more than once. Handing in forms at two stations, or applying both on paper and online, can disqualify you.
  • Missing documents. A form without the ID or matric copy the advert asked for is usually not considered at all.
  • Blank sections. Leaving questions empty makes the form look incomplete. Write "N/A" where something does not apply.
  • Arriving unfit. The fitness assessment is announced in every advert. Turning up without training is the most common reason good candidates drop out.
  • Hiding information. Leaving out a past conviction, a dismissal or a medical condition almost always comes out during vetting, and dishonesty is treated more seriously than the original issue.
  • Missing the closing date. Late applications are not accepted, even by a day.
  • Paying someone. Anyone who asks for money to help you get in is running a scam. See our guide on how to spot job scams.

How to prepare before the next intake opens

The best applicants start getting ready long before the advert appears. If you want to apply in the next intake, use the months before it to put yourself in the strongest position possible.

  • Get your documents ready now. Find your ID, matric certificate and any qualifications. If anything is lost, apply for replacements early, because it can take weeks. Our guide to certified copies explains how to get documents certified properly if the advert asks for it.
  • Train for the fitness test. Build up gradually and consistently. Running, push-ups and sit-ups three or four times a week for a few months makes a real difference.
  • Check your health. If you know your BMI or waist measurement is above the limits in recent adverts, start working on it well ahead of time, and see a clinic about any condition that might affect training.
  • Keep your record clean. Any criminal case, even a minor one, can rule you out. Stay away from situations that could lead to one.
  • Practise for the interview. Think of real examples from school, work, church, sport or community life where you showed honesty, courage, responsibility or teamwork.
  • Learn about the job. Speak to police officers you know, read SAPS news and understand what station work involves, including shifts and transfers.
