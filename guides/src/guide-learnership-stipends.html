Learnerships are one of the best ways to get a recognised qualification and real workplace experience at the same time. They are also one of the few routes into work that pay you while you learn. But "stipend" and "allowance" can mean very different amounts from one learnership to the next, and many applicants are unsure what is fair or legal.

This guide explains how learnership allowances work in South Africa, the legal minimum rates, what affects the amount you receive and how to budget while you are on a learnership. For the basics of what a learnership is and how it differs from an internship or apprenticeship, see our learnerships guide.

Allowance, stipend or salary?

You will see different words in adverts:

  • Allowance is the term used in the rules for learnerships. It is money paid to the learner during the learnership to cover costs such as transport and meals and to support them while they learn.
  • Stipend is often used informally to mean the same thing, and is also commonly used for internships.
  • Salary applies if you are already an employee of the company and do the learnership as part of your job. In that case, you keep earning your normal salary.

This guide focuses on the most common case: unemployed people who join a learnership and receive an allowance.

The legal minimum allowances

South Africa's national minimum wage rules include a schedule that sets minimum weekly allowances for learners on learnerships. The minimum depends on two things:

  1. The NQF level of the learnership qualification.
  2. How many credits you have already completed on the learnership. As you complete more credits, the minimum rises.

The rates are updated when the national minimum wage is reviewed. From 1 March 2026, the weekly minimums include, for example:

Rates change from time to time, so check the current schedule or ask the employer, training provider or SETA if you need the exact figure for your band.

Many employers pay moreThe legal amounts are minimums. Many companies, especially large employers and those in technical sectors, pay learners more than the minimum. Your learnership agreement and the advert state the actual allowance.

What affects your allowance

  • The level of the learnership. A higher NQF level usually means a higher minimum allowance.
  • Your progress. The minimum rises as you complete more credits.
  • The employer. Some employers top up allowances above the minimum, for example to match their entry-level pay.
  • SETA funding. Many learnerships are partly funded by SETA grants, which help employers cover allowances and training. This can affect how generous an allowance is.
  • The sector. Engineering, mining and finance learnerships often pay more than general learnerships.

Tax and deductions

Learnership allowances are generally subject to the normal tax rules, but most allowances are below the annual income tax threshold, so many learners pay no income tax. Whether UIF is deducted depends on your status under the learnership agreement. Your payslip or payment advice should show what, if anything, has been deducted. If you have questions about a deduction, ask the employer's HR or payroll office.

Learnerships can also bring tax benefits for employers, through incentives for registered learnership agreements. This is one reason companies run them, and it has no direct effect on your allowance.

What else a learnership gives you

The allowance is only part of the value. A good learnership also gives you:

  • A nationally recognised qualification registered on the NQF.
  • Workplace experience that you can list on your CV, with a reference from your supervisor.
  • Training paid for by the employer or SETA, not by you.
  • For some learnerships, a route to a trade test or professional registration. See our artisan guide.
  • A real chance of being employed by the same company afterwards, although this is not guaranteed.

Budgeting on a learnership allowance

A learnership allowance, especially at the lower bands, is not a lot of money. Before you accept a learnership, think practically:

  • Transport. Work out what it will cost to travel to the workplace and the training venue every day. This is often the biggest cost.
  • Meals. Plan for lunches, or take food from home.
  • Data and airtime. You may need them for training material and communication.
  • Grant implications. If you or your household receive social grants, find out whether the allowance affects eligibility.

If the allowance cannot cover your transport and basic costs, look for a learnership closer to home or speak to the training provider about your situation before you sign.

Warning signs: learnership scams

Because learnerships are so popular, they are a common target for scams. A genuine learnership:

  • Never charges you a fee to apply, register, attend training or receive your allowance.
  • Has a written learnership agreement signed by you, the employer and the training provider.
  • Is linked to a registered qualification and, usually, a SETA.
  • Pays your allowance into your own bank account.

Be very careful of anyone who asks you for money, or who offers to pay you an allowance only if you sign over your banking details or a portion of the money. Our guides to checking a job advert and job scams explain the warning signs.
