How to Become a Traffic Officer in South Africa

Traffic officers keep South Africa's roads safe, and trainee posts attract thousands of applicants. Here is how the career works and how to give yourself the best chance.

In short

  • Traffic officers are employed by municipalities and provincial traffic departments, not by SAPS.
  • Most trainee posts ask for Grade 12, a valid driver's licence and a clean criminal record.
  • Trainees attend a traffic training college and must pass the course before they can work as officers.
  • Applications go to the municipality or province that advertised the post, never through agents or paid 'registration'.

Every day, traffic officers stand at roadblocks, check vehicles for roadworthiness, weigh trucks, investigate crashes, manage traffic at events and enforce the speed limits that save lives. It is visible, responsible work, it comes with uniform and authority, and trainee intakes regularly attract thousands of applications for a handful of places.

Because demand is so high, traffic officer posts are also a favourite subject for scams. Fake adverts promise "guaranteed placement at traffic college" for a fee. This guide explains how the career really works: who employs traffic officers, what the requirements usually are, what training involves and how to apply safely.

What traffic officers do

Traffic officers enforce road traffic law. Their work includes:

  • Monitoring speed and issuing fines for traffic offences.
  • Running roadblocks and vehicle checkpoints, checking licences, roadworthiness and loads.
  • Testing drivers for alcohol and arresting drunk drivers.
  • Controlling traffic at crash scenes, events and where traffic lights are out.
  • Weighing heavy vehicles at weighbridges and checking that they comply with load limits.
  • Escorting abnormal loads and VIP convoys in some departments.
  • Road safety education at schools and in communities.
  • Giving evidence in court about offences they dealt with.

Some traffic officers also work as examiners of driving licences or examiners of vehicles at licensing and testing centres. These are separate qualifications, and adverts for examiners ask for them specifically.

Who employs traffic officers

Traffic officers are not employed by the South African Police Service. They work for:

  • Local and district municipalities, which run traffic departments for their towns and roads.
  • Metropolitan municipalities, where traffic policing is usually part of the metro police department, such as the Johannesburg, Tshwane, Ekurhuleni, Cape Town or eThekwini metro police.
  • Provincial traffic departments, which patrol provincial roads and national highways within the province, often under the provincial Department of Transport or Community Safety.
  • The Road Traffic Management Corporation (RTMC), the national body that coordinates road traffic law enforcement, which employs some officers and oversees national training standards.

This matters for your job search. There is no single national intake. Each municipality and province advertises its own trainee posts, at different times and with slightly different requirements.

Usual requirements for trainee traffic officers

Requirements differ between employers, but trainee adverts commonly ask for:

  • Grade 12 (matric) or an equivalent NQF Level 4 qualification.
  • A valid driver's licence, usually at least code B or EB, and sometimes a heavier code such as C1. Some adverts ask that you have held the licence for a minimum period.
  • South African citizenship and, often, residence within the municipality or province that is hiring.
  • A clean criminal record, with no pending cases.
  • An age range for trainee intakes, stated in the advert.
  • Physical and medical fitness, confirmed through assessments.
  • Computer literacy and good communication skills, because officers write reports and deal with the public in more than one language.

Where a municipality wants to appoint already qualified officers, the advert will ask for a completed traffic officer qualification, registration as a traffic officer, and sometimes a number of years of experience. Read carefully which kind of post you are looking at: a "Trainee Traffic Officer" post is for people who will be trained, while a "Traffic Officer" or "Senior Traffic Officer" post usually requires the qualification already.

Your driver's licence must be validAn expired licence card or a learner's licence will not meet the requirement. If your card is close to expiry, renew it before you apply, and keep a copy of the renewal receipt if the new card has not arrived yet.

The selection process

Because so many people apply, selection for trainee posts often has several stages:

  1. Screening. Applications are checked against the minimum requirements. Missing documents or an incomplete form usually mean you are out at this stage.
  2. Written assessment. Some employers test language, numeracy, general knowledge and road traffic signs.
  3. Physical fitness test. This may include running, push-ups, sit-ups and other exercises, with minimum standards.
  4. Interview. A panel asks about your motivation, your understanding of the role and how you would handle situations such as a motorist offering a bribe.
  5. Medical and psychometric assessments, depending on the employer.
  6. Background checks, including criminal record and qualification verification.

Start getting fit well before you apply. Physical tests eliminate many candidates who meet every paper requirement but have not prepared.

Training at traffic college

Successful trainees are sent to a traffic training college. Several provinces and large metros run their own colleges, and training follows national standards for traffic officers. Training typically includes:

  • Road traffic law, including the National Road Traffic Act and its regulations, and the procedures for issuing fines and making arrests.
  • Criminal law and procedure relevant to traffic policing, and how to give evidence in court.
  • Practical traffic control, roadblocks, vehicle inspection and speed law enforcement.
  • Crash scene management and accident reporting.
  • Firearm training, since many traffic officers carry firearms on duty.
  • Physical training and drill.
  • Ethics and customer service.

Training usually lasts several months and is assessed. Trainees who do not pass may not be appointed as officers, so treat the college seriously. After qualifying, new officers are registered as traffic officers and start working under supervision.

How to apply safely

  1. Find the official advert. Look on the website of the municipality, metro or provincial department, in its local newspapers and on official notice boards. OnboardingSA lists posts from official government sources with a link to the original.
  2. Check the application method. Municipalities often use their own application form or ask for a CV and covering letter; provincial departments usually ask for the Z83. Use exactly what the advert asks for.
  3. Prepare your documents. Usually your CV, ID, matric certificate and driver's licence. If certified copies are requested, our certified copies guide explains how to get them right.
  4. Deliver or submit before the closing date, to the exact address or portal named.
  5. Keep proof of submission, such as a stamped acknowledgement or email confirmation.

Never pay anyone to apply, to secure a place at traffic college or to be put on a "list". Genuine municipalities and provinces do not sell posts. Pay and career path

Traffic officer pay depends on the employer, because municipalities set their own salary scales within local government agreements, while provincial officers are paid on public service salary levels. Adverts state the salary or the level. Beyond the basic salary, permanent posts often include benefits such as a pension fund, medical aid subsidy and, in some cases, shift or standby allowances.

With experience, traffic officers can move up to senior traffic officer, sergeant, inspector, superintendent and chief roles, or specialise in areas such as crash investigation, weighbridge operations, by-law enforcement, training or road safety education. Some go on to qualify as examiners of driving licences or vehicles.

Preparing to stand out

  • Get your driver's licence early and keep a clean driving record.
  • Build your fitness now: run regularly and practise push-ups and sit-ups.
  • Learn the K53 rules and road signs thoroughly. They come up in assessments and interviews.
  • Volunteer at community safety or scholar patrol programmes if you can. It shows commitment to road safety.
  • Prepare honest answers about integrity. Panels often ask what you would do if a motorist offered you money, or if a colleague did.

Questions people ask

Is a traffic officer the same as a police officer?

No. Traffic officers enforce road traffic law and are employed by municipalities and provincial governments. Police officers are employed by SAPS and enforce criminal law more broadly. Metro police officers in the big cities do both traffic and crime prevention work within their municipality.

Which driver's licence do I need?

Most trainee adverts ask for at least a valid code B (or EB) licence, and some ask for a heavier code such as C1. The advert states the exact code, so check it before you apply.

Do I have to pay for traffic college?

When you are appointed as a trainee by a municipality or province, the employer normally arranges your training. Be very careful of anyone selling places at a traffic college or promising appointment for a fee. Genuine trainee posts never require payment to apply.

How long is traffic officer training?

Training at a traffic college usually runs for several months and includes theory, law, practical road work and firearm training. The exact length depends on the qualification and the college, and the advert or your employer will tell you.
